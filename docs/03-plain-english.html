The jargon, in plain English

Six things I need to be able to explain without notes. Not because the panel needs teaching, but because a Data PM who can only say these in jargon cannot take them to a client, a finance director or a board.

Why this page exists

The brief asks me to reason in four registers — product, engineering, commercial and governance — "rather than retreating into the one you find most comfortable". Translating technical facts into commercial consequences is that skill. Each item below ends with "so what", because that is the part that matters.

1. The join key — and why it is not a technical detail

You asked a fair question: if the panel does not want technical detail, why does a join key belong in my answer at all?

Because it is not a technical detail. It is the reason the product has any value.

Plain terms

Two lists. One from Plentific: "we fixed the boiler at 14 Mill Road on 3 March, took two visits, cost £340." One from the landlord's own system: "the tenant at 14 Mill Road is 82, has a respiratory condition, and is three months in arrears."

Separately, each list is mildly interesting. Together they answer "are we failing our most vulnerable tenants?" — which is the question the Regulator, the board and the press all care about.

The join key is whatever lets you line those two rows up and know they are the same home. Without it you have two lists and no way to match them. With a bad one you match the wrong rows, which is worse than not matching at all.

Addresses are a terrible join key — "Flat 2, 14 Mill Rd" and "14 Mill Road, Apt 2" are the same home and no computer knows it. That is why the UK has the UPRN: the Unique Property Reference Number, a national identifier for every addressable property. It is the closest thing to a universal home ID, and it appears in Plentific's data on the location record.

So what

  • The client is not buying Plentific's data. They are buying the ability to combine it with their own. That is the product. The join key is what makes combination possible, so it is a commercial fact wearing technical clothes.
  • It changes what "day one" means. A first release with perfect repair data and an unreliable join key delivers nothing, because nothing can be joined. A first release with less data but a dependable key is immediately useful. That is a scoping decision, and it is mine to make.
  • There is a real risk sitting in it. Plentific's own documentation says uprn is populated only for UK-namespace organisations and is otherwise empty. If it is patchy in practice, the product can quietly fail at its core purpose while every dashboard says the pipeline succeeded. I would want the fill rate measured before I promise anything.
  • It is also a data quality product opportunity. If Plentific commits to populating and validating UPRN properly, that is genuinely valuable to the client and hard for them to do themselves. That is worth charging for.
How I would say it in the room, in one sentence

"The client's whole reason for wanting this is to join our data to theirs, so the identifier that makes joining possible is the single most important field in the product — and today it is nullable."

2. "We only added a field" — why that can still break a client

This is the one I would most want to land, so here it is slowly.

There are two completely different ways of consuming data, and the same change affects them differently.

An app reading an API

Asks a specific question, gets an answer, uses the bits it needs, throws the rest away.

"What is the status of job 4471?" → "booked" → show a badge saying Booked.

If Plentific adds five new fields, the app never notices. It was not looking at them.

A warehouse copying data

Copies everything, every night, into a table with a fixed shape, then runs rules over it that assume the shape.

"Copy all jobs into jobs, which has 31 columns, then calculate our on-time percentage."

If the shape changes, the copy or the calculation can fail — or worse, quietly change meaning.

The analogy I would use with a client

Adding a field is like adding a paragraph to a letter.

Someone reading the letter for one specific fact does not care — their fact is still there.

Someone photocopying the letter every night into a binder, whose filing rule says "this letter has four paragraphs and the date is always in paragraph three", now has a broken process. Nobody wrote a worse letter. The copying rule just stopped being true.

The real example, using Plentific's own change

On 4 February 2026, Plentific added six new booking statuses: cancelled, arrived, in_progress, attended, not_attended, no_access.

Perfectly sensible product work — more precise tracking of what happened on a visit. For an app, completely harmless: it just displays whichever status it receives.

Now picture a client's warehouse. Somewhere in it there is a rule that translates status into a business meaning, written when there were four statuses:

  • completed → counts as a successful visit
  • cancelled_by_tenant → does not count against us
  • anything else → treat as not yet done

Six new statuses arrive. attended and no_access fall through to "anything else" and are counted as not yet done. The client's completion rate drops. Their "failed access" figure vanishes into a general bucket.

And here is the part that actually matters

Nothing errors. Nothing alerts. No pipeline fails. The numbers are simply wrong.

And in this sector those numbers do not just sit in a dashboard. They go into a Tenant Satisfaction Measures return to the Regulator, and into a board report, and potentially into evidence about whether a statutory deadline was met.

So the failure is not "a client's pipeline broke at 3am". It is "a client reported a wrong number to their regulator and neither of us knew". A pipeline that breaks loudly is annoying. A number that is quietly wrong is a liability.

So what

  • "Breaking change" has to be redefined for this product. The API definition — roughly "we did not remove or rename anything" — is not sufficient. For a warehouse consumer, a new category value, a new status, a changed default or a widened field can all change the meaning of a number.
  • Silent wrongness is the real risk, not outages. That points the design towards publishing meaning, not just structure: a documented list of every valid status value, versioned, so the client can detect a new one arriving instead of absorbing it invisibly.
  • It reframes the ask on engineering. I am not asking teams to stop adding statuses. I am asking that a new status be declared at the boundary. That is a far smaller and far more winnable request than "slow down", which the brief tells me is off the table anyway.
  • It also tells me what to refuse to commit to. I can promise the shape will not change without notice. I should be much more careful about promising that the set of possible values will never grow.

3. "There is already a change-capture primitive in the product"

Apologies — that sentence was jargon on stilts. Unpacking it:

  • Change capture means knowing what changed, rather than looking at everything and working it out.
  • Primitive means a basic building block that already exists, that bigger things can be built from.

So the sentence means: Plentific already has a working mechanism that announces the moment something changes.

Concretely, they have webhooks. A client registers an address and Plentific calls it the instant something happens. Real events from their documentation: work_order.job_hired, work_order.lead_rejected, compliance.document_processed.

Plain terms

Two ways to keep track of a shop's stock.

Recount everything every night. Slow, expensive, and you only ever know last night's position. This is what a nightly full export is.

Have the till tell you every time something sells. Cheap, instant, and you always know the current position. This is change capture.

Plentific already has the till. It is wired up and clients already use it.

So what

  • It is the foundation of the pushback. When a client says "give us a nightly full export", I can respond with something better rather than something cheaper: we already know the moment a job changes, so we can send you changes as they happen. Nightly full copies are the slowest and most expensive way to get you a worse answer.
  • It reduces the size of the build. I am not proposing a change-tracking capability from scratch. Part of the machinery exists. That matters enormously for the brief's hardest question — how to fund internal platform work with no client-visible value.
  • It carries a real limitation I should be honest about. Webhooks tell you a job changed; they are not designed to guarantee that a warehouse ends up with a complete, correct copy. If one call is missed at 3am, the client's copy is silently wrong forever. So events are a strong foundation, not a finished answer — you still need a way to reconcile.

4. Bulk import exists. Bulk export does not. Why?

You spotted the right thing, and you also correctly challenged my wording. Let me separate what is fact from what is inference.

What is fact

The published Client API has a Bulk Import capability: list bulk imports, create or update lots of records at once, check import status. There is no equivalent bulk export in the published documentation.

Why import exists

Because it solves Plentific's problem, not the client's. You cannot onboard Notting Hill Genesis by typing in 66,000 properties. Bulk loading is on the critical path to getting a client live and therefore to revenue. It got built because it had to.

Bulk export was never on the critical path to anything. So it did not get built.

The line I would use

Plentific built the road in and never built the road out. That asymmetry is the actual gap in this case study — not a missing feature, but a direction of travel that was never designed.

"But didn't they say they do bulk export?"

Worth reading the brief carefully here, because it says something more specific.

Bulk export appears in the brief twice, in two different senses:

  1. As what a client asks for: "the request arrives as 'can you batch export everything into our warehouse'" — that is the request, not a capability.
  2. As bespoke work: the brief says custom dashboards and "one-off extracts" get "quoted case by case".
So here is what I think is actually happening today

Bulk export does happen. It just is not a product. It is a person — probably an analyst or an engineer — writing a query against the internal warehouse, producing a file, and someone quoting for the time.

That reframes the case study usefully. This is not a greenfield build. It is productising something that is currently being delivered manually, at unmeasured cost, with no repeatability and no contract. The brief practically says so: "It works, at low volume, at unmeasured cost, with no commercial model."

Which gives me a much better first question than any architecture question: how many of these have we done, how long did each take, and who did them? That is the baseline for both the business case and the cost model, and somebody at Plentific knows it.

And you were right that there may be another way out

There is at least one route the public API does not show. Plentific integrates with client housing systems — NEC, MRI, Salesforce, Microsoft Dynamics. Those integrations move real volumes of data into client systems today.

They are point-to-point and built per system rather than being a general data product, but they mean data already leaves Plentific in bulk in some form. I do not know their terms, their cost, or who maintains them. That is a question for the room, not a conclusion for a slide.

5. "Bearer token with a 36,000 second expiry"

36,000 seconds is 10 hours. A "bearer token" is a temporary pass.

Plain terms

You arrive at a building and show your ID at reception. They give you a wristband. For the rest of the day you just show the wristband — nobody re-checks your ID. After 10 hours the wristband expires and you go back to reception.

Anyone holding the wristband gets in. That is what "bearer" means: the pass proves nothing about who you are, only that you have it.

In Plentific's case: an application sends its client ID and secret, receives a token valid for 10 hours, and attaches that token to every request until it expires.

So what

  • It is normal, and that is the point. This is a standard app-to-app arrangement. It is designed for an application doing work, which is exactly what the brief says the API is for — integration and workflow, not analytical extraction.
  • It signals the access model, and the model is app-shaped. The credential belongs to the client organisation and, as documented, comes with a single broad scope. There is no visible way to say "this credential may read repair history but must never see resident contact details".
  • That is a governance gap, and it will surface in a security review. A client's information security team will ask: can our analytics team have read-only access to a defined subset, separate from our integration team's write access? On what I can see today, the answer looks like no — it is one credential for the whole organisation.
  • It supports the argument for a separate channel. Any access model for the data product needs purpose-limited, read-only, revocable credentials that can be granted to a specific team. If that does not exist today, it is internal platform work — and it is precisely the kind of unglamorous work the brief asks me to sequence and fund first.

Caveat: I can only see what the public documentation shows. Finer-grained scoping may well exist internally. I would ask rather than assert.

6. Can we rely on these docs being up to date?

No, and Plentific say so themselves. Their G-Cloud service listing states, about the public API:

Plentific's own words

"as a general principle we seek to provide public API endpoints to mirror all functionality available within the platform. Note that this is an ongoing project and we don't currently guarantee parity between the two."

That single sentence tells me three things:

  1. The platform is bigger than the documentation. Absence from the docs proves nothing.
  2. They know it and have said so publicly. This is a known, accepted gap, not an accident.
  3. Parity is already an aspiration they hold. Which is useful — a data product contract is the same discipline pointed at a different channel.

I found a small corroborating example. A version, 2025-01-01, is live and receiving changes according to the August 2026 changelog, yet does not appear in the documentation navigation. So the docs do drift from reality.

So what

  • Every conclusion I draw from the API is a floor. I say that explicitly on my assumptions slide rather than being caught by it.
  • I never say "Plentific cannot do X". I say "X is not in the public documentation, so I have assumed it is not productised — please correct me". That is a stronger position and an honest one.
  • It is itself a finding. Documentation drifting from reality is a mild problem for an API used by integrators who can experiment. It is a severe problem for a data product, where the documentation is the product for a client's data engineer who cannot see inside. If we sell a schema, the schema documentation has to be generated, not maintained by hand.

One-line cheat sheet

What to say if asked, in one sentence each.
Term Plain English Why it matters commercially
Join key / UPRN The shared identifier that lets you match a row in our data to a row in theirs. UPRN is the national ID for a UK home. It is the whole reason they want the product. Nullable today, so the core value can silently fail.
Schema The agreed shape of the data: which tables, which columns, what type each one is. Once a client builds on it, it stops being documentation and becomes a promise.
Pipeline The automated job that moves data from us into their warehouse on a schedule. When it breaks, they cannot tell whose fault it is, and they call us either way.
Change capture Being told what changed, instead of recounting everything. Already partly exists via webhooks. Makes the nightly-full-export request answerable with something better.
Additive change Adding something without removing anything. Safe for an app. Can silently change a number for a warehouse.
Breaking change A change that stops the other side working, or changes what a number means. The definition itself is the product decision. Ours must be stricter than the API's.
Bearer token A temporary pass. Ours lasts 10 hours. Whoever holds it gets in. Built for apps, not for a client's data team needing narrow read-only access.
Reference data The lookup lists that turn identifiers into words — what each category and status means. Without it the export is barcodes. Easy to forget, and it changes constantly.
Restatement Correcting data the client already copied and already reported on. The brief asks about this directly. It is the hardest promise to make, because it means telling a client a number they filed was wrong.
Tenancy isolation Making certain one client can never see another client's data. The brief admits this has "never had to withstand a client's scrutiny". It is the first gate in any security review.
