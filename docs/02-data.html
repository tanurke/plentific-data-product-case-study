Step 2 · Discovery

What data exists, and how fast it moves

Everything Plentific holds, described in everyday language, with a view on which customer would want each part and why. Then the uncomfortable bit: evidence of how often it changes underneath them.

When was it last updated?

This turned out to be the most useful question I asked, because there are three different answers and they matter for different reasons.

The finding

Plentific's API changed on 10 September 2026 — the same day I prepared these notes.

The most recent entry in their public changelog adds a new uprn field to job location details in the Contractor API. Not last quarter. Not "recently". That day.

So no, the API was not last updated a long time ago. The brief says "Plentific ships continuously" and asks me to take that constraint seriously. The public changelog is the receipt.

Three different meanings of "last updated", and which one I can actually verify.
Which "last updated"? What it means Answer Confidence
The client's own data How fresh is the repairs information inside Plentific right now? Continuous. Plentific sells on "real-time repairs visibility" and has live webhooks that fire when a job changes. The operational data is essentially always current. High from their own marketing and the events in the API
The shape of the data When did the structure — the fields and tables — last change? 10 September 2026. And before that 26 August, 24 August, 20 August, 29 July. High published changelog
The internal warehouse How often does Plentific's own analytics warehouse refresh? Unknown. Nothing public. This is a question I would ask in the first five minutes, because it sets the floor on any freshness promise I can sell. None assumption to validate

That third row matters commercially. If the internal warehouse refreshes once a day at 2am, I cannot sell an hourly client feed regardless of what mechanism I choose. The promise I can make to a client is capped by the platform underneath it.

The insight I would open the presentation with

Look at the "kind of change" column. Almost every single entry is "we added something". Nine out of the last ten.

The brief presents continuous shipping as the thing that makes a data product impossible. But Plentific's own evidence says the opposite: they already absorb weekly product change into roughly twenty published, mostly additive changes a year, against a stable dated contract. The newest version clients are pointed at is still 2023-02-01 — a contract that has held for over three years while the product changed every week.

Plentific has already solved this problem once, for the API. The question is not "can we do this" — it is "can we extend a capability we already have to a new channel". That is a much cheaper and much more fundable argument, and it is the one I want to make.

This is the part everyone forgets, and it decides whether the export is usable at all

The part everyone forgets: reference data

This is a small point that turns out to be a large one.

Plentific's data is keyed on UUIDs — long identifiers like 15791638-f098-49ca-b1a6-40da5bc47255. A repair job does not say "gas boiler repair". It says category_uuid: 23791438-f098-....

In plain terms

Imagine being handed a spreadsheet of a hospital's activity where every column contains a barcode instead of a word. Ten thousand rows, perfectly accurate, completely useless — because the sheet that says which barcode means "X-ray" was not included.

So a client who asks for "raw data" and receives only the transaction tables gets barcodes. To make it usable they also need the lookup tables: the category tree, the status meanings, the location types, the service definitions, the staff list.

Two consequences worth carrying forward. First, the reference data has to be in scope from day one, and it is the part most likely to be forgotten in a first release. Second, those lookup tables are exactly where Plentific's configuration churns — new categories and new statuses get added constantly. So the least glamorous part of the export is also the part most likely to change under the client.

Five versions, and what that proves

Plentific's API is versioned by date. There are five versions visible:

Client API versions, with the size of each surface as I counted it from the rendered specification.
Version Groups of things Operations Status What I read into it
2020-01-01 20 73 Published, ageing Six years old and still documented. Somebody is presumably still on it
2021-10-28 29 105 Published Steady growth — roughly a third more surface in under two years
2023-02-01 47 180 Published, current This is what "latest" points to. Stable for over three years while the product shipped weekly
2025-01-01 37 138 Live but not in the docs menu The August 2026 changelog applies changes to it, so it is real and maintained — yet clients browsing the docs would not find it
2026-02-01 22 57 Exists, looks partial Much smaller, and the naming has changed again. Reads like a deliberate rebuild in progress

I would flag that fourth row as a question rather than a criticism: a version that is live and receiving changes but absent from the documentation navigation is exactly the kind of gap that erodes trust with a client's data team. If I have misread it, I would want to be told.

They renamed the core entity. Twice.

This is the most valuable thing I found, because it turns an abstract worry into something that demonstrably already happened.

Comparing the oldest published version to the current one, the central noun of the entire business changed:

How the core domain model was renamed between API versions.
In 2020-01-01 In 2023-02-01 and later What it is in real life
Properties Locations A home
Landlords Owners Who owns the home
Property Groups Location Groups A set of homes grouped together
Property Managers Location Managers The staff member responsible
Blocks, Estates Location Types Two separate concepts collapsed into one with a type field
And then again, more recently: Tenants became Residents in 2026-02-01.

A lovely detail that makes the point better than any argument: in the current specification, the uprn field sits next to an internal schema component still named Components_properties-name. The old internal name leaked into the public contract and is still visible today.

Why I care about this so much

These are exactly the changes that would silently destroy a client's warehouse. If a client had built pipelines reading properties and tenants, those pipelines would have stopped dead.

But here is the thing: it happened anyway, and nobody's warehouse broke — because the dated version boundary absorbed it. Old clients kept reading 2020-01-01 with properties in it. New clients got locations.

That is a working, proven answer to the hardest question in the brief, and it already exists inside the company. When the panel asks "how would you handle a breaking change at our pace of shipping", I want to answer with their own history, not with a theory.

The sensitive fields, and where they actually are

Not general data protection talk. These specific fields are in the published API today, which means they are in the platform and almost certainly in the warehouse.

Sensitive data found in the published Client API specification.
Field or area What it holds Why it is a problem Risk
vulnerable_resident, pair_visit Flags on the home indicating a vulnerable resident lives there, or that staff should visit in pairs A vulnerability flag attached to an address lets you infer health or disability. Awaab's Law requires landlords to hold this to triage hazards, so it cannot simply be removed Highest
Free text everywhere description, notes, access_notes, messages. Real example from their own docs: "No parking available at this property." Free text cannot be reliably masked. Staff type whatever helps them do the job, including things about people's health, behaviour and safety. This is the hardest single governance problem and it has no clean technical fix Highest
Photographs before_attachments, during_attachments, after_attachments, inspection images Photographs taken inside people's homes. Impossible to redact at scale, and expensive to move — images dominate both storage and transfer cost Highest
Contractor bank details sort_code, account_number, iban, bic_code, cis_number Third-party financial data. The landlord has no business holding a small contractor's bank details in their analytics warehouse Refuse

Signatures completion_declaration_signature A captured signature is biometric-adjacent and is evidence. Needs deliberate handling either way Restrict
Resident contact details Names, email addresses, phone numbers, full addresses Ordinary personal data, but at scale and joined to repair history it becomes a rich profile Minimise

Can I trust any of this?

Fair question, and the honest answer is: only partly. I want to say this in the room before anyone asks, because it is a credibility point rather than a weakness.

How much weight I put on each source.
Source How much I trust it Why
The changelog dates High Published, dated, specific. Hard to be wrong about "a field was added on this date"
Which entities exist High as a floor If the API documents it, it exists. But the reverse does not hold — see below
Which entities do not exist Low Plentific's own G-Cloud listing says they "don't currently guarantee parity" between the API and the platform. The platform is bigger than the docs
Anything about the warehouse None Entirely internal. Its technology, refresh cadence, modelling and cost base are all unknown to me
Anything about cost or contracts None The brief tells me there is "no established view of what it costs us to serve", so it may be partly unknown internally too
How I use this rather than hide it

The public API is a floor, not a ceiling. The internal warehouse almost certainly holds more than the API exposes — and one thing I would specifically expect is data the API does not show: the point-to-point integrations into client housing systems like NEC, MRI, Salesforce and Dynamics.

Which raises a question worth asking out loud: if we already move bulk data into client systems through those integrations, is the warehouse request a genuinely new product, or an existing capability that was never productised? I do not know. But I would rather arrive with that question than with a confident answer built on a documentation gap.

What this step tells me

  1. The change velocity is real but the change character is mild. Nine of the last ten changes were purely additive. The scary version of the constraint is not what the evidence shows.
  2. Plentific already runs a versioned data contract that has held for three years. This is the strongest asset in the whole case study and the brief does not mention it.
  3. The crown jewel is the repair job status history, because it is the legal timeline. If I had to ship one table first, it would be that one.
  4. Reference data is not optional. Without the lookup tables the export is barcodes.
  5. The hardest governance problems are free text and photographs, and neither has a clean technical solution. That pushes the answer towards contractual boundaries and deliberate exclusion, not clever masking.
  6. Some of what a client would receive is not theirs — contractor bank details and marketplace pricing. "Export everything" is not a request that can be granted as stated.

Open questions I would take into the room

  • How often does the internal warehouse refresh today? This caps every promise I can make.
  • What is it built on? Build-versus-buy on the sharing mechanism hinges on the answer.
  • Is 2025-01-01 deliberately unlisted, or has the documentation drifted?
  • How complete is uprn in practice? It is documented as populated only for UK-namespace organisations, otherwise empty.
  • Do the existing housing-system integrations already move bulk data out, and under what commercial terms?
  • When Properties became Locations, what did that actually cost in support and migration effort? That number is the best available estimate for what the boundary costs to maintain.
