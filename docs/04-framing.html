Step 4 · Framing

How I frame the problem

A course correction I made partway through, and the rule that came out of it: the brief is the specification, and everything I found in the developer documentation is only evidence.

The discipline check

The trap I nearly walked into

I spent a productive few hours in Plentific's API documentation and found genuinely useful things — the version history, the entity renames, the changelog cadence. It was easy to start building the presentation around that research.

That would have been a mistake. The panel is not assessing whether I can read an OpenAPI specification. They asked a product question: what should Plentific build, in what order, and what should we sell?

An impressive API analysis that does not answer that question is a failed presentation.

So the rule for the rest of this work:

The brief drives the structure

Every section of my presentation exists because the brief asks for it. If I cannot point at the sentence in the brief that a slide answers, the slide comes out.

The research earns credibility

The technical findings appear as supporting evidence inside those sections — the specific example instead of the generic claim. Never as the spine.

The practical difference is large. Compare two ways of answering the same question:

Same knowledge, two very different answers.
Research-led (wrong) Brief-led, research-supported (right)
Opening "Let me walk you through what I found in your API documentation." "Here is what I would build first, and why. And here is the thing in your own history that makes me confident it will hold."
On change control "You have five API versions and a changelog with roughly 70 entries." "You have already held one data contract stable for three years while shipping weekly, including through renaming your core entity. I would extend that discipline rather than invent a new one."
What it signals Thorough, but has not formed a point of view Has a point of view, and it is grounded in their reality rather than a framework

The second version uses exactly the same research. It just refuses to make the research the subject.

What the brief actually asks

Reduced to its demands, so I can check my answer against it:

The core question

What should Plentific build, in what order, and what should we sell?

With the explicit instruction that they are "less interested in a reference architecture diagram than in the decisions you would take and the ones you would defer, and why".

Four areas to work through, with a stated preference for depth over even coverage, and a request that I explain how I weighted them:

  1. Cost and commercial modelling — unit economics, then a view on the model. Explicitly: margin that works at ten clients and fails at a hundred is not a product.
  2. Technical considerations — mechanism and trade-offs, clients on different warehouse technologies and clients with none, what we model versus hand over raw, backfill, failure and recovery, build versus buy.
  3. Version and change control at their pace of shipping — flagged as "the part that quietly decides whether the product survives" and the one they "will push hardest on".
  4. Governance, security and data protection — tenancy isolation, access control, personal data, minimisation, retention, auditability, and where responsibility transfers to the client.

Then three things to cover across all of it:

  • The product and the sequence — what is the first shippable version, what comes next, what is explicitly out of scope, and how the internal platform work gets funded when it delivers no client-visible value on its own.
  • Making it real — validating demand beyond the loudest client, pricing and packaging, how success is measured for both sides, what goes to engineering first, and how a rollout phases from first client to general availability.
  • Where I would push back — if part of the batch export request is the wrong answer, say so, and say how I would have that conversation with a client who has already written "nightly full export" into their requirements.

And two standing instructions: state assumptions, and name trade-offs.

How I weight the four areas

The brief asks me to declare this, so I do it on a slide rather than leaving it implicit.

My weighting, and the reasoning behind it.
Area Weight Why I weighted it there
Version and change control Heaviest They told me they will push hardest here, and they are right to. It is also where I have the strongest evidence, because they have already done this once successfully. Leading with my strongest material on their stated priority is the obvious choice.
Cost and commercial Heavy It is the area most often skipped by product candidates, and the brief is pointed about it. It is also the only area where a wrong answer kills the product regardless of how good the engineering is.
Governance and security Heavy Weighted up from where I first placed it, for one reason: in this sector governance is not a compliance tax, it is the product boundary. It decides which rows and columns exist at all, so it has to be settled before scope can be.
Technical Lightest Deliberate, and I will say so. The brief explicitly deprioritises reference architecture. The mechanism choice is also the most reversible decision on the list — and the least reversible ones deserve the airtime.
The line that carries the weighting

"I weighted the mechanism lightest because it is the decision we can change later. Change control and governance are the ones we cannot — once a client has built on our schema and their security team has signed off, both become very expensive to revisit."

The bottom two rows are the discipline working. They were interesting to find and they are not worth a minute of a 40-minute session. They go in an appendix so I can answer if pushed, and stay out of the narrative if not.

The time budget

The logistics matter more than they look. The brief says 30 to 40 minutes including discussion, that they will "spend a good portion of it digging into your choices", and to "leave room for that rather than over-filling whatever you bring".

Read plainly, that is an instruction to bring less. So:

Planned shape of a 35-minute session.
Minutes What happens
0 – 2 The problem restated in my own words, and my headline recommendation. No build-up.
2 – 4 Assumptions and how I weighted the four areas. Short, and it buys credibility for everything after.
4 – 8 Who this is for, what they actually need, and where I push back on the nightly full export.
8 – 14 Change control. The heaviest section, built on their own history.
14 – 18 The sequence: internal platform work first, then first shippable version, then what is explicitly not being built yet.
18 – 22 Commercial model and governance boundary.
22 – 35 Discussion. Roughly a third of the session, protected on purpose.

That is around 20 minutes of material. It feels thin written down, and I think that is correct: the brief says the discussion is where they assess me, so over-filling the front half actively costs marks.

What I deliberately leave out

  • A reference architecture diagram as a centrepiece. The brief says they are less interested in it. One simple diagram to support a decision, not a slide that is the answer.
  • A named vendor recommendation. Build versus buy is a real question, but committing to a specific product without knowing what their warehouse runs on would be confidently wrong.
  • A precise cost model with invented numbers. I will bring the structure of the unit economics and the sensitivities that decide the answer, and be explicit that the inputs need their real figures.
  • A full API analysis. Appendix.
  • Anything resembling a plan to slow engineering down. The brief closes that door twice. Any proposal of mine has to work at their current pace, not ask for a different one.
  • Certainty I do not have. Where I do not know, I say so and say what I would do to find out.

What Step 5 has to produce

With discovery done, the next step is the actual product argument. It has to deliver four things, and nothing else:

  1. A one-sentence problem statement that is about the client's job, not about export mechanics.
  2. A clear target segment, with the segments I am consciously not serving in version one and why that is acceptable.
  3. The pushback, in words I could actually say to a client who has already written "nightly full export" into their requirements — offering something better rather than something cheaper.
  4. The success measures, for Plentific and for the client, defined before any solution so they cannot be quietly reverse-engineered to flatter the design.
Where I have got to

Four steps in, with no solution proposed. That is the intended outcome. I now know who this is for, what exists, what it is called, how fast it moves, and what the brief is actually asking — which means Step 5 can form a point of view rather than a guess.
