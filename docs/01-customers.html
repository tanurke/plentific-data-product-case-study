Step 1 · Discovery

Who the customers are

Before anything else: who pays Plentific, who actually uses it, and who the data is about. Those are three different groups, and confusing them is how a data product ends up illegal.

First, what Plentific actually does

In one sentence: when something breaks in a rented home, Plentific runs everything that happens next.

A tenant reports a broken boiler. Plentific logs the job, works out what kind of repair it is, finds a contractor who can do it (either the landlord's own in-house team or a local firm from Plentific's vetted marketplace), books the appointment, tracks the visit, collects the photographs and paperwork that prove the work was done, and handles the invoice. It does the same for legally required safety checks — gas, electrical, fire, damp and mould.

So Plentific's customers are landlords. Specifically, UK social landlords: the organisations that own and rent out affordable housing. Over 100 of them.

The basic questions

Who actually hands Plentific money?

UK social housing landlords. Three flavours of them: housing associations (not-for-profit landlords that own affordable homes), councils (local government, which also owns housing), and ALMOs (a company a council sets up to run the council's housing for it). They are all landlords. They differ mainly in who they answer to and how they buy software.

Are they all asking for this warehouse thing?

No, and this matters. The brief says demand is "concentrated in large housing associations with their own data teams and their own warehouse." That is a small slice of the 100-plus customers — the biggest ones. A council with 8,000 homes and no data team is not in this conversation at all.

So one of the first things I would want to establish is: how many clients is this actually, really? Is it fifteen large associations, or is it one very persuasive one? The brief warns me about "the loudest client" for a reason.

What is a "data warehouse" and why do they have one?

A single central copy of all of an organisation's data, in one place, so they can ask questions that cross systems. A large landlord runs several separate systems — one for tenancies and rent, one for finance, one for customer contact, and Plentific for repairs. Each one only knows its own corner.

A warehouse is how they get all of it into one room so they can ask a question like: "of the homes where we have had three or more damp repairs in two years, how many are occupied by someone we have recorded as vulnerable, and what have we spent on them?" No single system can answer that. Plentific certainly cannot — it does not know about tenancies or rent.

So what are they really asking Plentific for?

They are not asking for a report. They are asking to make Plentific one ingredient among several, so they can combine it with their own data and answer questions Plentific will never think to build a dashboard for. The brief says it directly: they want to be "one source alongside their housing management system, their finance system and their CRM."

Is the tenant a customer?

No — and this is the single most important distinction on this page. Tenants and residents use Plentific (they report repairs, they get appointment texts), but they do not pay for it and they are not who the data product is sold to.

They are the people the data is about. Their homes, their repair history, their photographs, their recorded vulnerabilities. That makes them the group with the most at stake and the least say. Every governance question in this case study ultimately traces back to them.

Are contractors customers too?

Effectively yes. Plentific is a two-sided marketplace: landlords on one side, building trades on the other. Home Group alone runs its in-house team plus multiple large contractors plus 200-plus small local firms through Plentific. There is a whole separate Contractor API for them.

This creates a problem the brief does not raise. Contractors' commercially sensitive information — their prices, their quotes, even their bank details — sits in the same platform. If a landlord says "export everything", some of that "everything" belongs to a third party who never agreed to it.

The sentence I want to say out loud in the room

The data is mostly about residents and contractors, but it is being sold to landlords.

That is why "just export everything" cannot be the answer, and it is a governance argument rather than an engineering one. It means the very first product decision is not how we move the data — it is which rows and columns a landlord is entitled to receive at all.

The buyer just changed, and nobody said so

This is the point I found most interesting, and it is not in the brief.

Today Plentific is sold to housing operations: a Director of Repairs, a Head of Asset Management, a Head of Property Services. Those people care about jobs completed on the first visit, contractor performance and compliance deadlines. They evaluate software by watching it handle a repair.

A warehouse data product is bought by a completely different person: a Head of Data, Chief Data Officer or CIO. They evaluate it by reading a schema, testing a pipeline, and sending it to their information security team. They have never used the Plentific interface and do not care about it.

Practical consequence: this product has a sales and onboarding motion Plentific does not run today. It needs schema documentation, a security pack and a support path that can answer a data engineer's question. That is real cost, and it belongs in the commercial model.

Why they are asking now

Clients are not requesting warehouse feeds because they enjoy building pipelines. Two things changed in UK social housing regulation, and both convert repairs data into legal evidence.

Awaab's Law

Named after Awaab Ishak, a two-year-old who died from prolonged mould exposure in social housing. It puts legally binding clocks on landlords:

  • Emergency hazards — investigate and make safe within 24 hours
  • Significant hazards — investigate within 10 working days
  • Written summary to the tenant within 3 working days of the investigation ending
  • Safety work within 5 working days of that investigation

Phase 1 came into force in October 2025 for emergencies and damp and mould. Phase 2 lands 30 November 2026 and extends it to a much wider range of hazards.

Mandatory returns to the Regulator

Landlords with 1,000 or more homes must file structured returns to the Regulator of Social Housing on fixed dates every year, through a portal called NROSH+:

  • Tenant Satisfaction Measures — due 30 June
  • Statistical Data Return — due 31 May
  • Local Authority Data Return — due 10 July
  • Quarterly survey and fire safety remediation returns — every quarter

Some of these measures are calculated from the landlord's own management information — which means from repairs data.

Put those together and the real driver becomes obvious. A landlord facing a 24-hour statutory clock needs to prove, later and to someone hostile, exactly when they were told, what they did and when. And the guidance explicitly requires them to hold records of tenant vulnerability, including physical and mental health conditions, in order to triage hazards correctly.

The reframe this gives me

The client says: "give us a nightly full export."

What they appear to actually need is: defensible, auditable, joined-up evidence that they met a legal deadline — combined with tenancy and vulnerability data that only they hold.

Those are not the same requirement, and the second one has implications the first does not. Evidence for a regulator needs to be correct as at a point in time and traceable. A nightly full overwrite destroys exactly that property: it silently replaces what you reported last month with what you believe today. I will come back to this in Step 5.

What this step tells me

  1. The paying customer is the landlord, but the data is about residents and contractors. The product boundary has to be drawn on entitlement, not on transport.
  2. The demand sits in one segment only — large associations with data teams. I need to size it honestly rather than take the loudest client's word for the market.
  3. There is a new buyer in the building. A Head of Data buys differently, churns differently, and costs more to support. That has to show up in the pricing.
  4. Councils and ALMOs will not follow the same path. Many have no warehouse at all, so any answer that only serves warehouse-owning clients leaves most of the base behind.
  5. The trigger is regulatory, not analytical. That changes what "good" looks like: auditability and point-in-time correctness matter more than freshness.

Open questions I would take into the room

  • How many clients have genuinely asked, in writing, versus how many we assume would want it?
  • Of those, how many have a warehouse today, and on which technology?
  • Has any client asked for contractor-level cost data specifically? That is the request I would refuse first.
  • For ALMO clients, who contractually owns the data — the ALMO or the council?
  • What did we already agree in contracts about data access, and to whom? The brief says data is "partly monetised" with no productised pricing, which suggests some clients may already believe they have bought this.
