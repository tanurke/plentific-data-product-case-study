The task, in my own words

Big clients want Plentific's repairs and compliance data inside their own data warehouse, so they can join it to their other systems and ask their own questions.

Plentific has a warehouse, but it was built for internal analytics. It is not modelled for outsiders, not governed to the standard needed, and not properly separated client-by-client.

So there are two products: the internal platform work that makes a client-facing data product possible, and the client-facing product itself. I have to sequence them and say what to sell.

What Plentific does

When something breaks in a rented home, Plentific runs everything that happens next: logs the job, finds a contractor (the landlord's own team or a local firm from its marketplace), books the appointment, tracks the visit, collects the photos and paperwork that prove it was done, and handles the invoice. It does the same for legally required safety checks — gas, electrical, fire, damp and mould.

Who the customers are

UK social landlords. Over 100 of them. Three types — but only the first type is asking for this.

Type What it is Examples Own warehouse? Asking for this?
Large housing association Big not-for-profit landlord, tens of thousands of homes, has its own data team Home Group, L&Q, Peabody, Notting Hill Genesis (66k homes), Southern Housing Yes Yes — this is the demand
Council Local government, owns council housing Southwark, Lambeth, Lewisham, Basildon Sometimes Mostly no. Wants compliance evidence, not analytics
ALMO A company a council set up to run its housing. Manages homes it does not own Northampton Partnership Homes Rarely No. And ownership of the data is genuinely unclear here

Also worth knowing: the buyer changes. Plentific sells to repairs and operations directors today. A warehouse feed is bought by a Head of Data, who judges it on schema docs, a reliability promise and a security review. Different person, different sale, more support cost.

One line to remember

The data is mostly about residents and contractors, but it is being sold to landlords.

So the first product decision is not how we move data. It is what a landlord is entitled to receive at all. That is why "export everything" cannot be granted as asked.

Why they are asking now

Not because they enjoy pipelines. UK regulation turned repairs data into legal evidence.

  • Awaab's Law. Legally binding clocks: investigate emergencies within 24 hours, significant hazards within 10 working days, written summary to the tenant within 3 working days. Phase 1 started Oct 2025. Phase 2 starts 30 November 2026 and covers far more hazards.
  • Annual returns to the Regulator. Landlords with 1,000+ homes must file fixed-date returns (Tenant Satisfaction Measures, Statistical Data Return and others). Some are calculated from their own repairs data.
  • The guidance also requires landlords to record tenant vulnerability, including health conditions, to triage hazards.
The reframe

They ask for: "a nightly full export."

They actually need: provable, auditable evidence that they met a legal deadline, joined to tenancy and vulnerability data only they hold.

Those are different. Evidence must be correct as at a point in time. A nightly full overwrite destroys exactly that — it silently replaces what they reported last month with what they believe today.

What data exists, and who wants it

Area What it actually is Who wants it, and why
Repair jobs Every job from reported to signed off. Includes a full history of every status change with timestamps Everyone. This is the crown jewel — the status history is the legal timeline Awaab's Law is judged against
Safety and compliance Gas, electrical, fire, damp checks. Inspection forms, answers, what was observed, follow-up work Compliance director — proving compliance and defending against enforcement

Why reference data matters

Plentific's data uses long ID codes, not words. A job does not say "gas boiler repair", it says category_uuid: 23791438-f098...

Hand over the jobs without the lookup lists and it is like handing someone a hospital spreadsheet where every column is a barcode. Perfectly accurate, completely useless.

What clients have today, and why each one breaks

  • Dashboards and reports. Fixed fields, cannot be changed. Breaks as soon as the client's definition of "completed repair" differs from Plentific's — which it always does, because the Regulator's differs too.
  • CSV downloads. Fixed columns, pulled by hand. Cannot be scheduled, cannot be joined, no schema promise, no audit trail. Useless as a pipeline source.
  • The public API. Capable, but built for workflow not bulk analytics. To rebuild a repairs table you would walk jobs, then bookings, then quotes, then invoices, then history, one at a time. Slow and expensive for both sides.

The pattern: all three are shaped by Plentific's definitions, but the client's questions are shaped by the Regulator's. That mismatch is the problem — not the transport mechanism.

The 6 facts that matter most for building a solution

  1. The API changed on 10 September 2026 — the day I researched this. They really do ship continuously. The constraint in the brief is real.
  2. But the change is mild. Only about 70 published changes in 3.5 years, and 9 of the last 10 were simply "we added a field". Not the chaos the brief implies.
  3. They already run dated versions (2020-01-01, 2021-10-28, 2023-02-01…), and the current one has been stable for over three years while the product shipped weekly. They have already solved this problem once, for their API.
  4. They already renamed their core entities and nobody broke. Properties became Locations, Landlords became Owners, and later Tenants became Residents. The version boundary absorbed it. This is my proof that the approach works — using their own history.
  5. Webhooks already exist. Plentific can already announce the moment a job changes. So I do not need to propose nightly full copies — I can offer something better.
  6. Bulk export already happens — manually. The brief says one-off extracts get "quoted case by case". So this is not greenfield. It is productising something already delivered by hand at unmeasured cost. Best first question to ask them: how many have we done, how long did each take, and who did them? That is the cost baseline and the business case.

The landmines

Landmine Why it is a problem
The join key is optional UPRN (the national ID for a UK home) is how clients match our data to theirs. It exists, but is often empty. So the product's whole purpose can fail while every dashboard says it worked
Free text Job descriptions, notes, access notes, messages. Staff type whatever helps them, including things about people's health and safety. Cannot be reliably masked. No clean technical fix — only exclusion or contract
Photographs Before/during/after photos taken inside people's homes. Impossible to redact at scale, and the biggest driver of storage and transfer cost
Vulnerability flags There is a vulnerable_resident flag on the home. Attached to an address, it lets you infer health or disability. And Awaab's Law requires it, so it cannot just be deleted
Contractor data Bank details, quotes, cost sheets. Not the landlord's data. In aggregate it is Plentific's own pricing intelligence
One credential per client Access is one broad credential per organisation. No way to give the data team narrow read-only access. A security review will ask about this immediately
Tenancy isolation is untested The brief admits it has "never had to withstand a client's scrutiny". First gate in any security review

The argument that wins the room

The brief says change control is what they will push hardest on. This is the point to make.

Adding a field is like adding a paragraph to a letter

Someone reading the letter for one fact does not care — their fact is still there.

Someone photocopying it every night into a binder, whose filing rule says "this letter has four paragraphs", now has a broken process. Nobody wrote a worse letter. The copying rule just stopped being true.

Real example: in Feb 2026 Plentific added six new booking statuses (arrived, no_access, not_attended and others). Harmless for an app. But a client's warehouse has a rule written when there were four statuses: "completed counts, cancelled does not, anything else is not yet done." The new statuses fall into "not yet done". Their completion rate drops.

The killer point

Nothing errors. Nothing alerts. The numbers are just wrong.

And here those numbers go into a return to the Regulator. So the failure is not "a client's pipeline broke at 3am". It is "a client reported a wrong number to their regulator and neither of us knew."

A loud break is annoying. A quiet wrong number is a liability. That is why "additive" for an API is not "additive" for a warehouse, and why my definition of a breaking change has to be stricter than theirs.

What the brief actually asks

My checklist. If something I prepare does not answer one of these, it comes out.

Core question: what should Plentific build, in what order, and what should we sell? They explicitly want decisions and deferrals, not a reference architecture.

Area The heart of it My weight
Change control How do clients depend on something stable while engineering keeps changing things? What counts as breaking? What do we commit to, and refuse to commit to? Heaviest They said they will push hardest here, and I have the best evidence
Cost and commercial What does it cost to serve, and what do we charge? Module, tier, usage-based? Margin must survive 100 clients, not just 10 Heavy
Governance Whose data is it? Tenancy isolation, personal data, what we withhold, and where responsibility passes to the client Heavy Here governance sets the product boundary, not just compliance
Technical How does the data get there? Files, warehouse sharing, managed pipeline. Clients on different tech, or none. Build or buy? Lightest Most reversible decision, and the brief deprioritises it

Plus three things to cover throughout:

  • The product and the sequence — first shippable version, what comes next, what is out of scope, and how the invisible internal work gets funded.
  • Making it real — validating demand beyond the loudest client, pricing, how success is measured, what goes to engineering first.
  • Where I push back — and how I would say it to a client who already wrote "nightly full export" into their requirements.

And always: state my assumptions, name the trade-offs.

Jargon, one line each

Term Plain English Why I care
Join key / UPRN The shared ID that matches a row in our data to a row in theirs. UPRN is the national ID for a UK home It is the whole reason they want this. And it is optional today
Schema The agreed shape of the data: which tables, which columns Once a client builds on it, it stops being documentation and becomes a promise
Pipeline The automated job that moves data into their warehouse on a schedule When it breaks they cannot tell whose fault it is, and they call us either way
Change capture Being told what changed, instead of recounting everything. Like a till telling you each sale, versus recounting the whole shop nightly Plentific already has this via webhooks. It is how I offer something better than a nightly export
Reference data The lookup lists that turn ID codes into words Without it the export is barcodes. And it changes constantly
Restatement Correcting data a client already copied and already reported on The hardest promise to make — it means telling a client a number they filed was wrong
Tenancy isolation Making certain one client can never see another's data First question in any security review. Currently untested
Bearer token A temporary pass, like a wristband. Show ID once, then just flash the band. Ours lasts 10 hours Built for apps doing work, not for a client's data team needing narrow read-only access

What I do not know, and would ask

  • How often does the internal warehouse refresh? This caps any freshness promise I can sell, whatever mechanism I pick.
  • How many clients have actually asked, in writing? Versus one persuasive client. The brief warns me about "the loudest client".
  • How many manual extracts have we already done, and how long did they take? This is my cost baseline.
  • What is the warehouse built on? Build-versus-buy depends entirely on the answer.
  • How complete is UPRN in reality?
  • What did the Properties-to-Locations rename actually cost in support and migration? Best estimate available for what maintaining a boundary costs.
  • For ALMO clients, who owns the data — the ALMO or the council?
  • What have we already promised in contracts? Data is "partly monetised" today, so some clients may believe they have already bought this.
Reminder to myself

The public docs are a floor, not a ceiling. Plentific's own G-Cloud listing says they "don't currently guarantee parity" between the API and the platform. So never say "Plentific cannot do X" — say "it is not in the public docs, so I assumed it is not productised, please correct me."

And the problem comes from the brief. The API research is only evidence that makes my examples specific instead of generic. It is never the story.
